Research & concept options — August 2026

A starship gamedrawn in light

First-person combat, a galaxy that moves while you're busy, and the phosphor-glow vector look the arcades left behind in 1983. Here's what's already been built, what's worth taking from it, and four ways to make the thing.

The brief, in three parts

what has to be true

01 · Battle, first person

You're behind the glass. Aim, manage shield facings, feel the hull take a hit — not a top-down icon exchanging numbers with another icon.

02 · Navigate & decide

A map with pressure on it. Where you go costs energy and time, and the situation degrades while you're heads-down in a fight somewhere else.

03 · Vector, not retro

Glowing strokes, additive blending, beam overshoot at the corners. Nostalgic in recipe, 2026 in execution — this is a shader problem, not a pixel-art one.

What's already been made

fifty-five years of prior art

This genre has been solved in pieces, repeatedly, by people working under severe constraints — and the pieces have never been assembled with modern rendering. The right-hand column is the shortlist of what to take.

YearTitleWhat it solvedTake
1971 Star TrekMike Mayfield, BASIC 8×8 quadrants of 8×8 sectors, a stardate deadline, energy and torpedoes as scarce currency, starbases to dock at. The deadline. Exploring is a gamble against a clock, not a free action.
1979 Star RaidersAtari 8-bit · Doug Neubauer The fusion: 3D cockpit for combat, 2D Galactic Chart for strategy, hyperwarp between them, subsystem damage, enemy fleets that advance in real time. The whole loop. This is the brief, already proven — and nobody has remade it.
1980–83 Battlezone · Tempest · Star WarsAtari, true vector CRT First-person wireframe combat with a radar; the colour XY monitor; the trench run. The entire visual vocabulary. Phosphor bloom. Overdriven lines, halo, beam overshoot — a post-process, not a model.
1982 Strategic Operations SimulatorSega arcade · Captain's Chair cabinet Split screen: forward view below for aiming, overhead scanner above for awareness. First licensed Trek arcade game, true vector. The split display. Solves "first-person needs spatial awareness" and looks superb.
1984 EliteBraben & Bell Wireframe 3D across eight procedural galaxies on 32K. Docking as a skill test worth failing. Scale from nothing. Lines carry a huge world cheaply.
1992 25th AnniversaryInterplay · episodic Away mission bookended by ship combat, then scored on how diplomatically you handled it. Shooting cost you points. Rewarding restraint. Almost nobody ships this, and it's the most on-theme mechanic there is.
1999–02 Starfleet Command · Bridge CommanderInterplay / Totally Games Tabletop-grade tactical combat — per-facing shields, energy allocation each tick — and giving orders to a crew instead of flying yourself. Energy budget & shield facings. A real decision every few seconds.
2010–17 Artemis · EmptyEpsilon · Bridge Crewopen source & VR Six players, one ship, one station each. Proved the bridge-crew fantasy has pull — and that it dies without a populated lobby. Station design. EmptyEpsilon is open source and worth reading.
2012–23 FTL · Duskers · NEBULOUSthe modern school Ship-as-systems-under-pressure; diegetic instruments instead of a HUD; fighting radar contacts rather than ships. Diegetic UI. Everything you read is something the ship is drawing — which is what vector wants anyway.
2018 Tempest 4000Llamasoft / Atari The vector look rebuilt with shaders at 4K/60. Proof the aesthetic reads as current, not as nostalgia. The reference render. This is the visual bar.

Four ways to build it

same pillars, different mixes
Option ARecommended

Deep Black

A modern Star Raiders. Real-time first-person cockpit combat welded to a live strategic chart, as 30–60 minute roguelike runs. Enemy fleets move on the map while you fight; docking to repair costs you ground.

Why
Exactly the brief, shape already proven, and no modern version exists.
Risk
Two systems that must pressure each other. Mitigated by building B first.
Scope
10–14 weeks to a strong vertical slice.
Option Bfastest to fun

Kobayashi

Pure vector arcade — the Sega 1982 split screen, escalating waves, a docking mini-game, a leaderboard. Five-minute sessions, no meta-layer. The aesthetic showcase, and A's first milestone either way.

Why
All the feel work, none of the strategy code. Ships in weeks.
Risk
Thin. If the feel isn't there, nothing else holds it up.
Scope
4–5 weeks to genuinely fun.
Option Cdeepest systems

Watch Officer

You command; you don't pilot. Viewscreen forward, stations around it. Order helm to a heading, tactical to target their weapons, engineering to shunt power to shields — and watch it execute with lag. Combat is budget and facing, not aim.

Why
Strongest captain fantasy; instruments are line drawings already. Co-op later.
Risk
"First person" becomes a viewscreen. Interface-heavy games take longer to make readable.
Scope
12–16 weeks, co-op a separate project.
Option Dmost distinctive

Long Survey

Exploration-forward. The 1971 quadrant grid as a place to investigate rather than clear: anomalies, derelicts, first contacts, distress calls, resolved as short vector-drawn encounters. Combat is punctuation, and the score rewards restraint.

Why
Nobody is making this, and encounters are cheap to write forever.
Risk
Furthest from "first-person battle". Needs real writing, which is a different production.
Scope
8 weeks for the frame, then content.

The call

sequencing

Build B, then grow it into A

They share a renderer, a flight model, a combat model and an art direction — B is A's cockpit with the chart removed. That order puts something playable and good-looking in front of you in about a month, proves the feel before we commit to the strategy layer, and de-risks the only genuinely hard part of A: making two layers apply pressure to each other.

Keep D's encounter system in the back pocket. It drops straight into A's chart later as "what you find when you warp into an empty sector" — which is exactly the content A will be short of.

  • wk 1–2Renderer. Wireframe pipeline, bloom, phosphor decay, stroke-drawn HUD glyphs. One ship rotating in a void that already looks right.
  • wk 3–5Kobayashi. Flight model, waves, shield facings, docking, leaderboard. Playable, shareable, honest arcade game.
  • wk 6–8The chart. 8×8 sectors, fleets that advance on a clock, hyperwarp in and out, energy as one shared currency.
  • wk 9–12The run. Starbases that can fall, subsystem damage that persists between fights, a win and a loss condition.
  • wk 13+Encounters. D's content system, seeded into empty sectors.

How the look is made

the recipe

TypeScript  +  Vite  +  Three.js (WebGL2)  +  WebAudio synthesis  —  no game engine, no asset pipeline, runs from a link

Geometry
Wireframe only, drawn with fat lines (LineSegments2) so strokes keep real width and don't dissolve at distance.
Glow
Bloom at a high threshold so only traces bloom, plus a second wide-radius low-strength pass for the halo.
Phosphor decay
Feedback buffer — blend the previous frame back at ~0.85 so fast motion smears the way a CRT does.
Beam artefacts
Brightness overshoot at line endpoints; corners hold the beam a moment longer than edges, exactly as an XY monitor did.
Colour
Additive blending throughout, so overlapping traces bloom to white where they cross. Cyan hull, amber alerts, magenta for the unknown.
Explosions
Break the model into its own line segments and fling them. No particles, no sprites — the ship comes apart into the strokes that drew it.
Type & HUD
Stroke-drawn glyphs on the same canvas, never DOM text, so every readout lives in one visual world.
Audio
Synthesised, not sampled — square-wave bleeps, filtered noise for warp, a drone that rises with alert level. Keeps the download tiny.

One caution before we name it

Paramount's published fan guidelines cover films — under 15 minutes, under $50k raised, non-commercial — and there is no games equivalent. Fan games using the marks have been shut down. The genre isn't protectable; the marks are. So: our own name, our own universe, no delta insignia, no LCARS layout, no familiar species. Everything in this dossier is mechanics and rendering, all of it fair game.
